So this plan replicates the archetype, not the artifact. A URL ending in /lab/ on a client-acquisition domain is, with high consistency, a free resource hub gated by an email address: a library of playbooks, prompts, teardowns and templates that gets added to on a schedule, positioned as an ongoing subscription of value rather than a one-shot lead magnet, and used as the top of a funnel that ends in a high-ticket done-for-you or coaching offer.

That structural read is what I’ve built against. Everything below stands on its own as a client-acquisition system regardless of whether their page matches — but the specifics I’d change with the real page in hand are listed in section 13.

Assumptions I’m making explicit

  • The Lab is free and email-gated, not paid. If theirs is a paid community, the whole front half of this plan changes shape.
  • Their money is made downstream of the Lab, in a high-ticket offer sold by application and call — not by self-serve checkout.
  • Their traffic is bought or borrowed (paid ads, affiliates, podcast placement) rather than purely organic. A go. subdomain is typically a funnel/ad-landing host, which points that way.
  • You are starting near zero: no meaningful list, no paid ad budget to burn, and time is your main input.
01

Define what $10k/month means

Three shapes hit the same number and demand completely different machines.

Shape A

1 × $10k

One anchor client. Fastest to the number, worst risk profile — one churn takes you to zero. Needs an enterprise-grade sales motion you probably don’t have yet.

Shape B — recommended

2 × $5k

Two retainers. Reachable in 90 days from cold, survivable if one churns, and $5k/mo is the price band where a solo operator or pair can still deliver excellently.

Shape C

4 × $2.5k

Most stable revenue, most delivery load. Four sets of calls, reports and expectations. Choose this only if delivery is genuinely productised.

Take Shape B, with a $2.5k entry tier as a landing pad. The entry tier isn’t a discount — it’s a scoped 30-day paid pilot that converts to $5k/mo on completion. It gives a hesitant buyer a way to say yes that isn’t “no,” and it gives you a proof asset within a month.

Write the target down as a dated commitment, because every number downstream derives from it: two $5k/month retainers signed within 90 days, with a third in pipeline.

02

The offer that carries the number

The list is only worth what the offer at the end of it converts at.

Most attempts at this fail here, not at traffic. A generic “AI consulting” offer converts at a fraction of a specific one, and no amount of list-building rescues it. Before you build a single page, lock three things.

The three locks

One ICP
Pick a vertical that passes all three tests: budget (they already spend >$5k/mo on the problem, in salary or tools), a repeatable AI-shaped pain (the same expensive manual workflow exists at every company in the vertical), and findability (you can build a list of 3,000 named contacts from public sources). Fail any one and move on. Do not serve two ICPs in the first 90 days.
One outcome
Price the result, never the hours. “We cut your [named workflow] from [N hours/week] to under [M], and you see the number in a weekly report” is sellable at $5k. “AI consulting and automation services, hourly” is not.
One proof asset
A free, specific, effortful teardown you can produce in 60–90 minutes for any prospect — a workflow audit that quantifies their cost of the problem in their own numbers. This is your entire sales mechanism. It doubles as the Lab’s headline offer and as the call-to-action of every cold email.

Choosing the vertical

Score three candidates against the tests above before committing — on paper, in an afternoon. Bias toward a vertical where you already have adjacent credibility or artifacts, because that collapses the proof problem. Whatever you already have running in production is a candidate ICP: the people who have that same problem are, by definition, findable and already spending on it.

The price conversation. $5k/mo only holds if the buyer’s cost of the problem is visibly north of $15k/mo. That is the audit’s job: not to demonstrate your cleverness, but to put a defensible dollar figure on their status quo. Once that number is on the table and they agree with it, $5k is arithmetic, not negotiation.

03

Build the Lab

Replicate the mechanic. Write your own words.

What makes a “Lab” different from an ordinary lead magnet is the recurring drop. A one-time PDF gets downloaded, filed and forgotten, and the subscriber goes cold in a week. A Lab promises something new on a schedule, which does three things a PDF can’t: it gives people a reason to keep opening, it keeps your deliverability healthy through sustained engagement, and it self-segments — which resource someone opens tells you which problem they have, and that’s the highest-signal targeting data you will ever get for free.

Build the page in this order. Each section has one job.

01 — Above the fold

Outcome headline naming the ICP and the transformation. One-line sub. A single email field and one button. No navigation — every link is a leak.

02 — Proof strip

Logos, a subscriber count, or one hard number. If you have none yet, use a specific credential sentence instead of a fake social-proof bar.

03 — What’s inside

Four to eight named assets, each with a one-line “what this saves you.” Specific titles outperform categories: a real filename beats “templates.”

04 — Who it’s for / not for

Explicit disqualification. Naming who should leave raises list quality and, counter-intuitively, raises opt-in rate among the people you want.

05 — The recurring hook

“New drop every Tuesday.” This is the section that makes it a Lab. Name the cadence and then never miss it.

06 — Why you

Short credibility block. One paragraph, one photo, one concrete thing you’ve shipped. Not a résumé.

07 — Second CTA + side door

Repeat the email capture, and add the high-intent path underneath: “Or if you’d rather we just run it — request an audit.” A slice of traffic is ready to buy on arrival.

08 — FAQ

Five or six real objections, answered plainly. “Is this actually free,” “will you spam me,” “is this for me if I’m non-technical.”

Structure is copyable. Copy is not.

Page architecture, funnel mechanics and offer structure are fair game and are not protectable. The actual sentences on their page are. Don’t lift headlines, body copy, or their asset names — rewrite everything in your own voice against your own ICP. It also converts better, because their words were tuned for their audience.

Target conversion: a cold-traffic Lab page should convert visitors to opt-ins at 25–40%. Below 25% the headline is wrong — not the button colour, the headline. Fix that before spending on traffic.

04

Build the list — five engines

Ordered by time-to-first-dollar, not by prestige.

“A 10k-a-month client list” has two honest readings, and you need both: a named prospect list of companies who could plausibly write a $5k/month cheque, and a nurtured subscriber list that produces inbound applications. Engine A builds the first. Engines C, D and E build the second. Engine B is what converts the first into revenue while the second is still compounding.

Engine A — The named account list weeks 1–3

This is the literal client list, and it is an asset you own forever. Target 3,000 verified contacts across ~1,200 accounts in your one ICP.

  • Account criteria: headcount band, revenue proxy, tech signals (what they run tells you what they’re missing), geography, and one trigger signal — a recent raise, a relevant job posting, a new tool adoption. Trigger-filtered contacts outperform static lists by a wide margin.
  • Contact criteria: the person who owns the pain, plus the person who owns the budget. Often not the same human. Sequence both, differently.
  • Build it in layers: a data provider for the base pull, enrichment for the trigger signals and firmographics, then verification to strip anything that will bounce. Never sequence an unverified address — bounce rate is the single fastest way to destroy a sending domain.
  • Own it: the list lives in your own CRM or warehouse, not inside a tool’s free tier. Tools churn; the list is the asset.
Engine B — Cold outbound to that list weeks 2–12, the cash engine

This is what pays for the time it takes the other engines to compound. It requires an unglamorous infrastructure build before the first send:

  • Secondary domains. Two or three lookalike domains, never your primary. Your main domain’s reputation is not a thing you gamble.
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC configured on all of them. Non-negotiable in 2026 — the major inbox providers enforce it for bulk senders.
  • 2–3 inboxes per domain, warmed for 14–21 days before real volume. Cap at 25–30 sends per inbox per day, permanently. Six inboxes gives you ~150 sends/day, which is the volume the math in section 7 assumes.
  • Four-step sequence: a short specific opener referencing the trigger signal; a value-forward follow-up that gives away something real; a case or number; a one-line breakup. Every step ends with the same low-friction ask — the free audit, not a meeting.
  • The ask is the audit, not the call. “Want the teardown?” converts several times better than “got 15 minutes?” because it costs the prospect nothing and puts you in the position of giving.

Pair each email sequence with a light LinkedIn touch on the same contact — a profile view and a connect, no pitch. Multi-channel lifts reply rate materially at near-zero cost.

Engine C — Organic content into the Lab weeks 1–12, compounding

One flagship artifact per week — a teardown, a build log, a benchmark with real numbers — published where your ICP already is. Then cut it into 4–5 short posts across the week. Every piece ends with the same Lab link.

The rule that makes this work: publish the actual thing, not a teaser for the thing. Give away the method; sell the implementation. People who can execute it themselves were never going to pay you; people who can’t now trust that you can.

Realistic yield from a standing start: 80–200 opt-ins/month by month three, growing. Slow at first, then it is the cheapest channel you have.

Engine D — Borrowed audiences weeks 3–12, highest ROI cold-start

The most underused channel at zero list size, because it skips the compounding period entirely. Four moves:

  • Podcast guesting. Pitch 20 shows that serve your ICP; expect 3–5 bookings. Each is worth 50–300 opt-ins if you give a show-specific Lab URL.
  • Newsletter swaps. Find non-competing operators writing to the same ICP. One well-matched swap can beat a month of posting.
  • Referral partners. Agencies, fractional CTOs and accountants adjacent to your ICP who see the pain but don’t solve it. A standing 10–15% referral fee, documented, is cheap client acquisition.
  • Communities. Answer real questions in the places your ICP already gathers. Never link-drop — be the most useful reply in the thread and let people find you.
Engine E — Paid only after organic converts

Do not start here. Paid traffic amplifies whatever your funnel already does — including failing. Turn it on only once the Lab page converts above 25% cold and at least one client has come through the nurture path, so you know what you’re buying.

Then: $50–100/day on the single channel where your ICP is most precisely targetable, driving to the Lab page, judged on cost per application — never cost per opt-in. If a client is worth $15k over its life, a $200 cost-per-application is excellent and a $12 cost-per-opt-in tells you nothing.

05

The nurture machine

A list you don’t email is a cost, not an asset.

Five welcome emails, then a weekly drop forever. That’s the whole system. The welcome sequence does the heavy lifting — it’s where engagement, segmentation and the first invitation all happen.

DayEmailIts actual job
0Access + the single best assetDeliver instantly. Ask one question: “what’s your #1 bottleneck right now?” Replies train the inbox providers that you’re wanted, and hand you segmentation data.
2The mechanismTeach how the thing actually works. No pitch. Establishes that you know more than the person who wrote their last vendor deck.
4Proof, with a numberOne story, one metric, one named constraint you worked around. Specific beats impressive.
7The failure modeWhy doing it in-house stalls at step four. Creates the gap between knowing and having, honestly, without disparaging anyone.
10The invitationThe free audit, with the application link. This is the only hard CTA in the sequence, which is exactly why it converts.
wkThe weekly dropNew Lab asset every week, same day. Every fourth issue carries the audit invitation in a footer, not a headline.

Segment on behaviour, not on form fields. Someone who opened three assets about the same problem is a hotter lead than someone who ticked a box six weeks ago. Tag on asset opens, and let a repeated-interest tag trigger a personal, plain-text, one-to-one email from you. That email — not the automation — is what books calls.

Prune ruthlessly. Anyone who hasn’t opened in 90 days gets one re-engagement attempt and is then suppressed. Carrying dead addresses degrades deliverability for the people who are reading, and a smaller engaged list outperforms a bigger cold one on every metric that matters.

06

Application to signed

Where the list becomes revenue.

Never send traffic straight to a calendar. An application form between the click and the booking is the highest-leverage filter in the whole system: it kills tyre-kickers, it forces the prospect to articulate their own pain in writing before they meet you, and it means you walk into every call already knowing the answer.

The seven questions

  1. Company, your role, and who else signs off on spend like this.
  2. Describe the workflow you want fixed, in your own words.
  3. How many hours a week does it consume, across how many people?
  4. What have you already tried, and where did it stall?
  5. What happens if this is still broken in six months?
  6. When do you want it working by?
  7. Budget band for this: under $2k/mo · $2–5k/mo · $5–10k/mo · $10k+/mo · not sure yet.

Question 7 does most of the work. Include the bands and it self-selects; omit them and you spend your calls discovering you were never going to agree.

The call, in 35 minutes

0–5 min
Context. Confirm what they wrote. Don’t re-ask what the form already answered — showing you read it is half the trust.
5–20 min
Diagnosis. Quantify the problem in their numbers, out loud, and get them to agree to the figure. This is the entire call. If you leave without a number both of you accept, there is nothing to price against.
20–30 min
The plan. Three phases, plain language, with what changes and how they’ll know. No slides.
30–35 min
Price and next step. Say the number on the call. Book the follow-up before you hang up.

Proposal inside 24 hours, three options, anchored high: a $10k/mo full engagement, the $5k/mo core retainer, and the $2.5k 30-day pilot. Most buyers take the middle. The high option isn’t decoration — occasionally someone takes it. Every option is a real thing you can deliver, and none of them are priced in hours.

08

Delivery, so it survives month three

$10k/month only counts if it’s still there in the quarter after.

Two clients at $5k means a single churn is a 50% revenue drop. Retention is not a nice-to-have at this size, it’s the difference between a business and a good quarter. Four mechanics carry it:

  • A first-14-days plan that produces one visible win. Whatever the smallest real result is, ship it inside two weeks. The relationship is decided early.
  • A weekly async update — short recorded walkthrough, three bullets, one ask. Cheaper than a meeting and it makes your work visible, which is what actually prevents churn. Invisible work looks like no work.
  • A monthly number. The same metric you quantified on the sales call, reported against its baseline. This is the renewal conversation, held every month so it’s never a conversation.
  • 90-day scoped terms that roll over. Not month-to-month (too easy to drift away), not annual (too hard to sign). Review at day 75; that’s also your expansion window.

The expansion trigger: around day 60, most clients surface a second workflow with the same shape as the first. That’s a scope conversation, not a favour. It’s also the cheapest revenue in the entire plan — no list, no funnel, no call.

WeeksBuildOutboundLab & contentTarget by end

The one sequencing rule

Inbox warmup starts on day one, before the offer is finalised, before the page is written. It is the only step with an unavoidable 14–21 day wait, and every day you delay it delays first revenue by a day. Everything else can be built in parallel while it runs.

Six numbers, reviewed every Monday, decided in advance.

Contacts sequenced250Capacity — add inboxes, not volume per inbox

Proposals out1Not diagnosing hard enough on the call to earn one

Decide these now, while it’s cheap

  • Under 1% positive replies after 500 well-verified contacts → change the ICP or the offer. Do not spend a third week rewriting subject lines.
  • Good reply rate but under 15% close after 8 held calls → the offer or the price is wrong, not the traffic. The diagnosis step is where to look first.
  • Lab page under 25% opt-in on cold traffic after 300 visitors → rewrite the headline against a sharper outcome, not the design.
  • No client by week 10 → stop adding channels. Go back to the two or three held calls that got furthest and find out, directly, what stopped them.

Write these thresholds down before you start. The whole point of deciding in advance is that in week seven you will be too invested to decide fairly.

12

Legal guardrails

Cheap to respect up front, expensive to retrofit.

  • US cold email (CAN-SPAM). Accurate headers and subject lines, a real physical postal address in every message, a working opt-out honoured within 10 business days. B2B cold email is lawful here when these hold.
  • EU/UK (GDPR & PECR). Materially stricter, and legitimate-interest grounds for cold B2B outreach are narrow and country-dependent. Simplest safe path for a 90-day sprint: exclude EU and UK contacts from outbound and reach them through content and inbound instead.
  • Canada (CASL). Consent-based, with real penalties. Exclude from cold sequences unless you have a documented basis.
  • Data sourcing. Use providers whose terms permit outreach use. Scraped-and-resold lists of unknown provenance are both a legal and a deliverability liability.
  • Copy. Replicate structure and mechanics freely; don’t reuse another site’s sentences, asset names or design assets. Rewrite everything.
  • Claims. If you publish results or income figures anywhere in the funnel, they must be substantiated and typical — the FTC treats unsubstantiated earnings claims as deceptive, and this niche gets looked at. Use your own real numbers, with context, or use none.

General guidance, not legal advice — run the outbound plan past a lawyer if you scale beyond a few thousand contacts or into regulated verticals.
